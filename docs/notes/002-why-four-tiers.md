title: Why Four Tiers
number: 002
summary: Why the Evidence Room sorts by epistemic status rather than by topic.

The Evidence Room does not sort exhibits by subject. It sorts them by how
well we actually know them:

- **What we know** — established, replicated, not seriously contested.
- **What the evidence suggests** — real findings pointing a direction, not yet
  settled.
- **What researchers disagree about** — where competent people read the same
  evidence and reach different conclusions.
- **What we don't know** — the question is open, and honestly so.

## Why this sorting and not another

Most presentations of science flatten these into one register. A textbook
sentence about memory consolidation and a contested claim about how memory
reconsolidates read identically on the page — same confident tone, same
paragraph, same weight. The reader has no way to tell which one a researcher
would defend and which one they would argue about.

Sorting by epistemic status puts that distinction back where it belongs: in
the structure, not buried in the hedging. Two claims about the same brain
region can sit in different tiers, and that difference is the most useful
thing on the page.

## The fourth tier is not the leftovers

The temptation with a scheme like this is to treat the last tier as a tidy
place for the offcuts — what didn't fit. It is the opposite. The fourth tier
is the reason the other three exist. The first three establish how far the
evidence reaches, so that the fourth can show where it stops.

A file that reaches the fourth tier has not failed. It has arrived.

## Where disagreement goes

The third tier is deliberately separate from the second. "The evidence
suggests" means the findings lean one way and nobody has strongly contested
it. "Researchers disagree" means the contest is live. Collapsing those two
would let a genuine scientific argument be presented as a mild uncertainty,
which is a distortion in the direction of false confidence — the exact
distortion this archive is built to resist.
